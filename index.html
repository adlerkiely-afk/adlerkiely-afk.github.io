About me

The short version

I'm a sophomore in aerospace engineering at Kent State. I started on the rocket team in January 2026 fabricating fiberglass fins, and by August I was leading the payload subsection. Now I'm responsible for other people's work as well as my own.

On my own time I'm designing SpeedUAS, a high-speed quadcopter. My first prints didn't fit. That taught me to measure the real part instead of trusting the datasheet, and you can read about it in the revision history below.

I also tutor calculus and physics, which mostly means breaking a hard problem into steps someone else can follow. Outside engineering, I've logged 40 hours of flight time in single-engine airplanes.

Getting two probes out with one servo

One unit of the team's 4U CubeSat-format weather station payload for the 2027 rocket. During descent, a single servo drives a symmetrical linkage that pushes the pitot probes out of the payload.

  1. Trade study
  2. Modeled in Onshape
  3. Being built
  4. Flies at IREC, summer 2027
The problem
Symmetric deployment from one servo, inside a CubeSat-format frame.
Trade study
Rack-and-pinion against a slider-crank, with stroke and torque sizing.
Change of plan
The probes were first meant to measure wind speed. We decided that reading could not be accurate, so we re-approached the design to measure descent speed, which gives the weather station more data against altitude.
My part
Directing a 6-member subsection through design, fabrication and integration.
Honest status
Not flown yet. No flight data.

April 2026 – present

A quadcopter shaped like a rocket

A 5-inch, 6S quadcopter with a streamlined fuselage. This first iteration is meant to validate the airframe and set a baseline for a second one.

  1. Modeled in SolidWorks
  2. Fit-check prints
  3. CFD meshing
  4. Assemble & fly
Fuselage
Myring body of revolution, 62 mm diameter. The battery sits forward and the flight electronics aft, which keeps the frontal area small.
Trade-off
Four integral NACA 0024 arms, 38 mm chord, with a carbon-fibre spar and internal wire channels. I considered a thinner NACA 0018 and rejected it: slightly less drag, but far less bending stiffness and no room for the spar and wiring.
Estimate
A thrust-equals-drag model predicts about 240 km/h against a 160–175 km/h target. It uses datasheet thrust and assumed drag coefficients, so it's an estimate, not a result.
Honest status
Not assembled or flown. The CFD is being meshed and hasn't been solved yet. The thrust stand is designed but hasn't been run.

Revision history

What the first SpeedUAS prints taught me
RevWhat went wrong, and the fixWhat I took from it
AThe battery didn't fitI modeled the pack from its datasheet size with about 1 mm of clearance. The real pack measured 34.9 × 38.7 × 80.7 mm. I increased the fuselage diameter and resized the cage to the measured part.Measure the real part before designing around it.
BI misread a datasheetI drew the motor bolt pattern as a 16 mm square, but the "16" was the bolt circle. Measuring the motor fixed the pattern and let me shrink the nacelles to the motor.Read what a dimension refers to, not just its value.
CA bridge that printed badlyThe motor plate had a 29 mm unsupported bridge. It's now a funnel-shaped cavity with screw pockets, and every part prints without supports.Design for the process that will make the part.

Next on SpeedUAS

Open items, in order

Fit-print the center tube

A pass/fail check of the bore, the stack plate, the insert holes and the tail fit.

Weigh every part

Replace datasheet masses with measured ones to get the first real all-up mass and thrust-to-weight figure.

Solve the CFD

Finish the volume mesh, solve the bare airframe, and replace the estimated drag area with a computed one. Then run the thrust stand.
